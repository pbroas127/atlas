# Training Plan — 8-Month-Old Bernese Mountain Dog (Newly Rehomed)

## Read this part first — it changes everything else

### 1. He is not "harder to teach because he's older." That's a myth.

Eight months is a *better* age to train than eight weeks. Longer attention span, better impulse control, better physical coordination. He can learn sit in about ten minutes.

The real obstacle isn't his age — it's that **he's been in your house for a short time and doesn't have a relationship with you yet.** Training is downstream of that. Fix the relationship and the cues come fast.

### 2. He's in decompression. Expect the "3–3–3" curve.

- **First 3 days:** overwhelmed, shut down, sleeping a lot or pacing. Not his real personality.
- **First 3 weeks:** settling in, learning the routine, real personality starts showing. Often when new "problems" appear — this is normal and means he's comfortable enough to act like himself.
- **First 3 months:** actually feels at home. Bonded. This is when you find out who he really is.

**Do not judge him or make decisions about him inside the first 3 weeks.** The calm-then-suddenly-crazy thing you're seeing is completely normal for both a decompressing dog *and* an adolescent — it's not a behavior problem.

### 3. He's in the second fear period, and this is time-sensitive.

Large breeds go through a second fear imprint period roughly **6–14 months**. He's dead center. During this window:

- New fears can appear overnight for no reason
- Things he was fine with last week can suddenly scare him
- **Anything you force him through right now can become a permanent fear**
- Conversely, careful positive exposure right now pays off for his entire life

This is the single most important reason to never drag, force, or flood him. You have maybe 4–6 months of high-leverage window here. Use it correctly and you get a bombproof adult dog. Force him and you get a 100lb dog with lasting fears.

### 4. Berners are a soft breed. Harsh handling backfires badly.

Bernese are notoriously handler-sensitive. They shut down under pressure rather than push back. A correction that a Lab shrugs off can cost you weeks with a Berner. Everything below is reward-based for that reason — it's not ideology, it's what works on this specific breed.

### 5. Get a vet check, specifically for pain.

Worth knowing: **noise sensitivity in adolescent large-breed dogs is correlated with undiagnosed musculoskeletal pain.** The dog associates a startle with a twinge and generalizes. Berners are prone to hip/elbow dysplasia and OCD lesions, and he's at the age where those show up.

Ask the vet to specifically check joints and do a pain assessment — not just "he seems fine." Rule this out before you spend three months on behavior work.

## Ground rules

**Never do these:**

- ❌ Drag or pull him through anything he's afraid of
- ❌ Alpha roll, pin, scruff, or "dominance" anything
- ❌ Prong collar or e-collar on a fearful adolescent
- ❌ Yell, or use his name angrily (his name must always predict good things)
- ❌ Punish a growl — a growl is information. Punish it and you get a dog that skips the warning
- ❌ Take things out of his mouth by force (creates resource guarding, and he's big enough that this matters)
- ❌ Force him to "face his fear." Flooding a fearful dog makes it worse, not better

**Always do these:**

- ✅ Let him choose to approach. Choice is what builds confidence
- ✅ Comfort him when he's scared. You **cannot** reinforce an emotion — the old "don't coddle" advice is wrong
- ✅ Let him retreat to a safe spot, always
- ✅ Keep sessions short and end while he's still winning
- ✅ Feed generously. He's a big dog with a big appetite — use it

## Setup

### Gear

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| **Y-front harness** (Ruffwear Front Range, Balance) | Removes throat pressure. A scared dog on a flat collar can back out and bolt — genuine safety issue |
| **Martingale collar** | Cannot be backed out of. Double-clip leash to harness *and* martingale for outdoor safety |
| **6ft flat leash** | No retractables — gives constant tension, which is the exact opposite of what he needs |
| **15–20ft long line** | For recall and letting him move at his own pace |
| **Treat pouch** | Speed matters. Fumbling in a pocket kills timing |
| **Baby gates / X-pen** | Management, not training. Covered below |
| **Lickmat + 3–4 food puzzles** | Adolescent brain drain |

### Currency

**Stop feeding him from a bowl for now.** Measure his daily ration and hand-feed or train with all of it. For a new dog this does more for the relationship in two weeks than anything else — you literally become the source of everything good.

Keep a three-tier treat system:
- **Tier 1** — kibble. Easy stuff in a quiet room.
- **Tier 2** — training treats, cheese. Normal work.
- **Tier 3** — hot dog, chicken, string cheese. **Reserved exclusively for scary things.** Never used for anything else.

Berners get fat easily and **lean body weight is the #1 thing you control for joint longevity in a giant breed.** Training food comes out of his meal ration, not on top of it.

### Session structure

- **3–5 minutes**, 4–6 times a day. Not one 30-minute session.
- **End before he's bored.** Always leave him wanting more.
- **One new thing at a time.**
- If he fails twice in a row, you went too fast. Back up a step.

### Marker word

Pick one word — **"yes"** — and use it the instant he does the right thing, then deliver food within about a second. The word is a camera shutter: it marks the exact moment that earned the reward. This is the mechanical skill that makes all the rest work, and most people are sloppy about it.

## The leash / doorway problem

This is the most specific and most fixable thing you described, so it gets its own section.

### What's actually happening

He's **fine once outside.** So it isn't the outdoors, it isn't walking, and it isn't stubbornness. Planting and pulling backward at the door means one of:

1. **The leash itself is the trigger** — it may have been paired with being grabbed or dragged before you got him
2. **The threshold is the trigger** — the doorway is a pressure point where noise, light, and novelty all hit at once
3. **Restraint is the trigger** — a dog never properly leash-conditioned as a puppy can find the loss of freedom itself aversive

You don't need to know which. The protocol is the same.

### The one rule

**If the leash is tight, you have gone too fast.** Every single rep should be on a loose leash. The moment you pull him, you've taught him that the leash means being overpowered, and you've set the work back.

### The protocol

Run each phase for 2–3 days minimum. Move on only when he's *happy* at the current phase, not merely tolerating it.

**Phase 1 — The leash predicts food (no walking at all)**
Pick the leash up → say "yes" → treat → put the leash back down. Repeat 15–20 times, a few times a day. That's the entire session. You're done when picking up the leash makes his ears perk and he trots over.

**Phase 2 — Clipping on means a party**
Clip it to the harness → immediately feed 5–6 treats in a row → unclip. No going anywhere. Then let him drag the leash around the house (supervised only) so it stops being a signal that something is about to happen to him.

**Phase 3 — Movement indoors**
Leash on, walk him around the living room and kitchen, feed for following. Still not going near the door.

**Phase 4 — The door exists**
Leash on, walk *toward* the door, feed, **turn around and walk away.** Do not open it. Repeat until walking toward the door is boring.

**Phase 5 — Open door, don't go out**
Open the door. Stand there. Feed Tier 3 treats. Close the door. Don't leave. Repeat until the open door is a food dispenser.

**Phase 6 — He crosses on his own**
Toss treats across the threshold so *he* chooses to step through. Never any leash tension. He crosses, gets paid, comes back in. Then across and stay out. Then across and walk.

**If he plants at any point:** stop. Don't pull. Stand still on a loose leash and wait, or toss a treat back toward yourself and let him reset. Then run an easier version. Planting = you skipped a step.

### Speed it up with a decoy

If you have another person, have them walk out the door ahead of him casually. Following a moving human is much easier for a nervous dog than deciding to go alone.

## Noise sensitivity

### The principle

Two things happening simultaneously:
- **Desensitization** — expose at an intensity so low he barely notices
- **Counter-conditioning** — pair that low intensity with Tier 3 food so the sound starts predicting chicken

Both fail if he's over threshold. **Over threshold** = won't take food, whale eye, tucked tail, panting, trying to leave, freezing. If he won't eat, you are too close or too loud. That's your gauge — no guessing required.

### Sound work (10 min/day)

1. Find a YouTube/Spotify sound library — thunder, fireworks, traffic, sirens, vacuum, doorbell.
2. Play it at a volume so low it's nearly inaudible.
3. Sound plays → chicken rains from the sky. Sound stops → chicken stops.
4. **Only the sound predicts food.** Silence must be boring. That contrast is what does the work.
5. Raise the volume by tiny increments *across weeks*. If he reacts at all, drop back two steps.

Ten minutes a day for two months will meaningfully change how he handles the world.

### Real-world noises

When something startles him:
- Don't say "it's okay" in a worried voice — say nothing tense, just be neutral and normal
- **Do** feed him immediately, and don't worry about "rewarding fear." You are pairing the noise with food, which is exactly the mechanism
- Let him leave. Never block his escape
- Never make him approach the scary thing

### A safe space that's always available

One spot — crate with the door open, bed in a quiet corner — that is never violated. Nobody reaches into it, nobody drags him out, no nail trims there. A dog with a reliable escape hatch gets braver, not more avoidant.

### The vacuum and the grooming dryer

You'll need both on a Berner for the next decade. Introduce them now, at 8 months, correctly:
- Week 1: appliance sits unplugged in the room. He gets fed near it.
- Week 2: someone runs it in a *different room with the door shut* while he eats.
- Week 3: same room, far away, on for 5 seconds, food the whole time.
- Week 4+: gradually closer, gradually longer.

Fire up a force dryer next to a nervous Berner on day one and you will never touch him with it again.

## The cues

### Priority order

1. **Name / attention** — nothing else works without it
2. **Hand target** — the most underrated cue, does half your work for you
3. **Sit**
4. **Drop it / trade** — this one is a safety cue
5. **Leave it**
6. **Place / mat** — the real answer to "trusted in the house"
7. **Off**
8. **Recall**
9. **Settle**
10. **Shake** — fun, and it doubles as paw-handling prep

---

### 1. Name / attention

Say his name **once**, in a light tone. The instant he looks — "yes," treat. That's it. 20 reps a day, in easy environments first.

Rules: never say his name twice. Never say it to interrupt something he's doing wrong. Never call him by name for anything unpleasant. His name means *"look at me, good things follow"* and nothing else. If you burn it, you can't get it back.

### 2. Hand target ("touch")

Hold a flat palm out a few inches from his nose. Curiosity makes him boop it. Mark "yes," treat.

This is worth learning first because it becomes a **remote control**: it moves him without touching him — off the couch, through the doorway, away from something, into the car, onto the scale at the vet. For a dog who doesn't want to be pushed or pulled, a cue that means "come put your nose here" is enormously valuable. Build it up to where he'll cross a room to target your hand.

### 3. Sit

**Capture it, don't push his butt down.** Physically manipulating a nervous dog costs you trust and teaches nothing.

- **Capturing:** stand there with treats. The moment he sits on his own — "yes," treat. He'll start offering sits to make you produce food. *Then* add the word.
- **Luring:** treat at his nose, move it slowly back over his head. His head goes up, his rear goes down. Mark the instant it hits the floor.

Fade the lure by rep 5–10 — otherwise you've taught "sit when there's food in your hand," which is a different and useless behavior. Add the word "sit" only once he's doing it reliably. Cue first, then behavior, then reward. Never repeat the cue.

### 4. Drop it / trade — the safety cue

**This is the most important cue on this list.** He's a big dog who will eventually pick up a chicken bone, a corn cob, or a sock. A sock obstruction is a $3,000–8,000 surgery and can kill him. "Drop it" is the cue that prevents that.

**Never chase him or pry his mouth open.** That teaches him that having something means losing it, which is how you manufacture resource guarding in a dog big enough to make it a real problem.

Build it:
1. Give him a boring toy.
2. Show him something *clearly* better — chicken.
3. He drops the toy. Say "drop" as he does. Give the chicken.
4. **Give the toy back.** This is the step everyone skips and it's the whole trick — dropping things has to be a good deal, not a loss.
5. Over weeks, work up to higher-value items.

Practice this constantly when the stakes are zero, so it's automatic when they aren't.

### 5. Leave it

Boring treat in a closed fist. He sniffs, licks, paws. Wait. The instant he backs off — "yes," and reward **from the other hand**. Never let him have the thing he left.

Progress: fist → open palm (close it if he lunges) → on the floor under your hand → on the floor uncovered → dropped as you walk past → real objects on walks.

### 6. Place / mat — the answer to house freedom

If you only teach one thing for living with a giant dog indoors, this is it. "Go to your bed and stay there" solves the doorbell, dinner time, guests, and the general problem of 100lbs of dog underfoot.

1. Put a mat down. Reward *any* interaction with it — looking, stepping on it.
2. Reward all four feet on it.
3. Reward lying down on it.
4. Feed repeatedly *while* he stays. Duration is built by paying him for staying, not by asking for more and more.
5. Add the cue "place."
6. Build distance, then duration, then distraction. **One at a time** — never increase two at once.

Goal: 20–30 minutes on the mat while you eat, work, or have people over. This takes a couple of months and is worth every session.

### 7. Off

"Off" = get four feet on the floor (couch, counter, person). Distinct from "down" (lie down). Don't use the same word for both.

Teach it with a hand target or a treat tossed to the floor, mark the moment his feet land, then name it. Do **not** knee him or push him — with a Berner that's both counterproductive and, at his adult size, unsafe for whoever he jumps on.

For jumping on people: he gets attention only when four feet are on the floor. All attention stops the instant he's up. Everyone in the house has to do this or it won't work.

### 8. Recall

Use a **brand-new word** — "here" or a whistle. Do not use his name, and do not use any word already worn out by being ignored.

- Start indoors, one foot away, party when he arrives.
- Long line in the yard next.
- **Every single recall gets paid, forever.** No exceptions.
- **Never call him for anything he dislikes** — nail trims, bath, crate, end of a walk. Go get him for those.
- Never call him if you aren't confident he'll come. Every ignored recall damages the cue.

### 9. Settle

Adolescent Berners genuinely need to be taught how to switch off — it's a skill, not a mood.

Leash him to your chair while you work or watch TV. Ignore him. When he lies down and sighs, calmly drop a treat between his paws. He learns that doing nothing is a paid job. Fifteen minutes a day. Massive payoff for a dog who cycles between calm and crazy.

### 10. Shake

Low priority for behavior, high value for **paw handling** — you're going to be trimming his pad hair and doing his nails for the next ten years, so paws being touched is a real skill.

Hold a treat in a closed fist near the floor. He paws at it. Mark and reward the paw lift. Then present an open hand instead. Then name it "shake."

Then extend it: touch the paw, treat. Hold the paw for one second, treat. Touch a nail clipper to a nail without cutting, treat. **Cooperative care** — he learns that handling predicts food and that he can opt out. Do this now and grooming is easy for his whole life.

## House freedom without the crate

### The honest version

**Freedom at 8 months, three weeks into a new home, is too early.** Not because of him — because of the math. Full house trust for a Berner typically lands **18–24 months**. He's an adolescent in an unfamiliar house. That's the highest-risk combination there is.

That's not a reason to keep him crated forever. It's a reason to do it as a gradual earn instead of a switch you flip.

### Don't abandon the crate — rehabilitate it

Even if he never sleeps in it again, he needs to be *okay* in one. Berners have real orthopedic and health issues; there will be vet stays, possible surgery recovery with strict crate rest, travel, emergencies. A dog who panics in a crate makes all of those far worse.

So: feed him in it, door open. Toss treats in, let him walk out. Stuffed Kong in there. Never use it as punishment. Make it a good room he can choose, and you keep the option without needing to use it daily.

### Management is not failure

**Every successful chew is a rep.** Chewing a table leg once makes chewing it twice more likely. You cannot train away a behavior he gets to keep practicing. So the goal is that he *never gets the chance* — not that you catch and correct him.

Tools, in order of how much freedom they give:

1. **Crate** — sleeping and unsupervised absences
2. **X-pen or one gated room** — kitchen with a tile floor and nothing chewable is ideal
3. **Tether / drag line** — he's loose but attached to you or a fixed point, so he's always within reach. Huge middle step
4. **Dog-proofed room, supervised**
5. **Dog-proofed room, you step out for 5 minutes**
6. **Multiple rooms, short absences**
7. **Full freedom**

### The ladder

Move to the next rung only after **two solid weeks with zero incidents** at the current one.

- **Weeks 1–4:** Crate or pen when unsupervised. Drag line when loose. You are watching him every second he has freedom.
- **Weeks 5–8:** One fully dog-proofed room. Leave for 5 minutes. Then 10. Then 20. Camera on him — you need to know what he does when you're gone, not what you assume.
- **Months 3–4:** That room for up to an hour. Add a second room.
- **Months 5–8:** Extend duration. Reassess honestly at every setback.
- **12–18 months:** Realistic target for meaningful unsupervised freedom.
- **One incident = drop back a rung, no drama.** This is normal and not a failure.

### Redirect, don't punish

When you catch him with something he shouldn't have: **don't yell, don't chase.** Trade him for something better (see "drop it"), then hand him an appropriate chew and praise him for taking it. You want the lesson to be *"chewing this thing gets me paid"* — not *"having things in my mouth near a human is dangerous."*

### Give him legal outlets

An adolescent Berner has a genuine physical need to chew. If you don't supply it he'll source it himself.

Rotate 5–6 items so they stay novel: bully sticks, yak chews, frozen stuffed Kongs, Nylabones, a Toppl. Frozen Kongs are the best value — they last, they're soothing, and they're perfect for the moment you leave the house.

Puppy-proof properly: cables in covers, shoes and socks behind doors, trash behind a cabinet latch, remotes and glasses up high. **Socks and underwear are the classic surgery items** — be strict about those specifically.

## Exercise (giant-breed rules)

His growth plates don't close until roughly **18 months**. Overdo it now and you buy him arthritis at five.

**Avoid until 18 months:** jogging on pavement, repetitive stair work, jumping out of cars or off furniture, forced long-distance walking, hard turns on slick floors.

**Do instead:**
- Sniffing walks — let him choose the pace and stop as much as he wants. Free-form sniffing tires a dog more than distance does
- Off-leash mooching on grass, at his own pace
- Swimming, if he likes it
- **Mental work** — a 10-minute training session or a food puzzle is worth a 45-minute walk for an adolescent brain

Also: **runners on your hardwood floors.** Big dog, slick floor, developing joints — this matters more than it sounds like it does.

## First 8 weeks

**Weeks 1–2 — Relationship and safety**
Hand-feed everything. Name, hand target, sit. Leash protocol phases 1–2. Start sound work. Crate stays positive. Strict management. Vet check including pain assessment. *No pressure on him for anything.*

**Weeks 3–4 — Foundation**
Add drop it and leave it. Leash protocol phases 3–4. Start place. Start settle. Sound work continues. Introduce vacuum/dryer at a distance.

**Weeks 5–6 — Application**
Leash protocol phases 5–6 — he should be walking out the door on his own. Place duration to 5 minutes. Recall on a long line. Begin the freedom ladder rung 2. Add "off" and "shake."

**Weeks 7–8 — Proofing**
Run cues in new rooms, then the yard, then on walks. Place duration to 15 minutes. Extend absences. Reassess sound thresholds.

**Reality check:** by week 8 he should know his cues in easy contexts and be walking out the door willingly. He will **not** be a finished dog. Berners mature slowly — expect real adult reliability somewhere between **18 months and 2 years.** You're building the dog he'll be at three.

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | What it means | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Won't take food | Over threshold — he is genuinely too stressed to eat | Increase distance / lower intensity. This is your single best gauge |
| Knows it at home, ignores you outside | Normal. Dogs don't generalize | Re-teach every cue in every new place, from step one |
| Was working, now isn't | Adolescent regression, or a new fear period | Back up two steps. Don't get frustrated. It passes |
| Sits and stares blankly | Shut down, or the criteria are unclear | Make it easier. End the session |
| Only performs with food visible | Lure was never faded | Go back, get the behavior first, *then* produce food |
| Great for 3 weeks, then problems | The 3-week mark — he's comfortable enough to be himself | Expected. Not a setback |

## When to bring in a professional

Get help if you see: any bite or air snap, guarding food or objects, panic-level fear rather than nervousness, or total refusal to leave the house.

Also just get a few sessions anyway. **This is the highest-value money on the entire list** — a giant, sensitive, fearful adolescent is exactly the case where good coaching pays for itself several times over, and where a bad trainer does lasting damage.

**Look for these credentials:** CCPDT (CPDT-KA), KPA-CTP, IAABC, Fear Free Certified, or a Veterinary Behaviorist (DACVB) for serious fear.

**Walk away from anyone who:** talks about dominance, alpha, or pack leadership; recommends a prong or e-collar for a fearful 8-month-old; guarantees results in a set number of sessions; or won't let you watch the training.

Berners are soft. A balanced/correction-based trainer can undo months of work on this specific dog in a single session.

## The short version

1. He's not hard to train — he just doesn't know you yet. Hand-feed him for a month.
2. Never pull him through a doorway. Make the leash predict chicken instead.
3. If he won't eat, he's too scared. Back off. That's the whole gauge.
4. He's in a fear period. Don't force anything for the next few months.
5. Manage the chewing so he can't practice it; earn freedom rung by rung.
6. Teach "drop it" like his life depends on it, because at some point it will.
7. Get the vet to check him for pain.
8. Everything you do wrong right now, you'll be doing it to a 100lb dog in six months. Everything you do right, you get for the next ten years.
